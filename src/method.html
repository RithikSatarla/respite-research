Method

How the benchmark was built

Why the faults are synthetic

None of these recordings comes with independent, human-checked labels of station faults. The development set's per-row training_valid flag is written by its own recorder; it is a useful reference but not ground truth. Respite's own earlier findings on this data are Respite's measurements, so scoring Respite against them would be circular.

So every detection number comes from faults written into real recordings, where the fault, its start and the affected episodes are known by construction. Development faults are applied in memory; test faults are written into copies of the MCAP files, so every tool reads the same bytes.

Detectors

  • Respite rules (shipped). respite live's station rules, unchanged: per-station status from the last five episodes, with a recorder rule (rows a second against the declared rate), a camera rule (new frames against rows, stale rows), a delay rule against the station's first episodes, and a motion rule. A problem must persist over three counted episodes before a warning.
  • Rules, one episode at a time. The same thresholds without the cross-episode logic. An ablation of Respite's own design.
  • Robust z baseline. Each measurement against the median and spread of the segment's first eight episodes; an alert when the median score of the last three episodes passes a threshold. No training.
  • Trained model. A per-episode classifier (no fault, or one of six categories) on the measurements, their change from the segment's first eight episodes, and missing-value flags. The recorder's own validity flag and episode length are not inputs. Station score: mean fault probability of the last three episodes.
  • HFlow 0.2.22 (Hebbian Robotics, open source). See the benchmark page: it could only be compared episode by episode.

Training

Two model families were tried, logistic regression (C in 0.1, 1, 10) and histogram gradient-boosted trees (depth 3 or unlimited, 100 or 300 iterations), with 4-fold cross-validation grouped by station and week of recording. The configuration with the highest out-of-fold station-level recall at the matched alert rate won. One model was trained per development draw: three draws of the synthetic faults, each with its own random seed.

Thresholds

The robust z threshold and each model's threshold were set on the development set so that their rate of alerts on unmodified recordings matched the shipped rules' rate there, {{ training.target_alerts_per_100h | f0 }} alert events per 100 station-hours. That rate is high because the development stations carry real problems the rules flag. The thresholds were then frozen with SHA-256 hashes before the test set was scored.

Scoring

  • Detected: the detector raises an alert event (off to on, or a change of category) at or after the first faulty episode. If it was already alerting at the start and raises nothing new, the fault counts as missed and is listed as masked.
  • Delay: recording minutes from the start of the first faulty episode to the end of the episode after which the alert came.
  • Alerts on unmodified recordings: alert events on control trials per 100 station-hours. Real recordings can hold real faults, so these are not called false positives.
  • Category: whether the alert names the right subsystem (camera, recorder, timing, missing stream).
  • Attribution: each faulty segment is paired with a healthy segment recorded "at the same time"; correct when the first alert is on the faulty one and the healthy one stays quiet.
  • Uncertainty: 95% bootstrap intervals over segments (2,000 resamples); differences between detectors are paired on the same resamples.

The decision rule was fixed in advance: a detector is called better than the shipped rules only if the paired interval of its recall minus the rules' recall is above zero and its alert rate on unmodified recordings is not higher.
